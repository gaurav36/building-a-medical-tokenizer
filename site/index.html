01 The starting point

Building a Medical Tokenizer

Build a vocabulary that knows your domain.

A general tokenizer can represent cardiomyopathy, but a domain-specific tokenizer may encode it more efficiently, using fewer subword pieces. The real work is building that tokenizer and measuring exactly what changes.

These notes follow medtok, the practical project currently named tokenization-explainer. You will prepare medical text, learn a vocabulary from scratch with Hugging Face tokenizers, compare it with a general-text control, and save a reusable artifact. The BPE engine comes from the library; the corpus, training recipe, and learned vocabulary are yours.

Why build a medical tokenizer?

A language model does not read raw text directly. A tokenizer first breaks text into smaller pieces called tokens, then maps those pieces to numerical token IDs that the model can process.

For example, a general-purpose tokenizer might represent cardiomyopathy as ["card", "iomy", "opathy"], while a tokenizer trained on medical text might learn a different split such as ["cardio", "myopathy"] or represent the whole term as a single token: ["cardiomyopathy"]. The exact result depends on the training corpus, vocabulary size, and tokenizer configuration.

The goal is not to make the tokenizer understand medicine. It is to learn a vocabulary that represents the target domain efficiently and consistently.

Why does tokenization matter?

Both examples preserve the same original word, but the second uses fewer pieces. A longer token sequence gives the model more tokens to process, consumes more of its context window, and may increase compute and memory costs. This makes token efficiency useful to measure: how many tokens are needed to represent text from the domain?

Fewer tokens are not automatically better answers. The tokenizer controls how text is represented; the model still has to learn the meaning of those representations and how they relate to one another.

Questions to answer

The purpose of this project is not simply to build a tokenizer and claim that it is better. Instead, we want to measure what actually changes when the tokenizer is trained on medical text.

1. Does a general tokenizer fragment medical text more?

Do medical terms, abbreviations, and units require more tokens when processed by a general-purpose tokenizer?

2. Does a medical tokenizer reduce token counts?

At a comparable vocabulary size, does training on medical text produce shorter token sequences on unseen medical documents? We should not evaluate the tokenizer only on the text it was trained on.

3. Does less fragmentation help the model?

A shorter representation does not automatically mean better language-model performance. Tokenizer efficiency must eventually be separated from downstream model performance.

4. What are the trade-offs?

A domain-specific vocabulary can have costs as well as benefits:

  • It may be less efficient on general English text.
  • It may require retraining or adapting a model.
  • Existing model checkpoints expect a particular tokenizer and vocabulary.
  • The tokenizer must be maintained as the domain or vocabulary changes.

Therefore, the goal is not simply medical tokenizer = better. We want to understand the trade-offs between domain efficiency and general-purpose coverage.

Why might medicine need its own vocabulary?

General-purpose tokenizers are trained on broad collections of text, so their vocabulary reflects the patterns that are frequent in that mixture. Medical text contains its own recurring structures: specialized terms, abbreviations, drug names, anatomical words, laboratory measurements, chemical names, units, and common prefixes and suffixes.

acetylcholinesterase
      cardiomyopathy
      electrocardiography
      immunohistochemistry

A sufficiently large and representative medical corpus may teach a tokenizer reusable pieces for patterns such as cardio, neuro, -itis, or -ectomy. BPE does not know that cardio relates to the heart; it learns only that the sequence occurs often enough to reuse.

How will we investigate it?

We will first use a small toy example to make BPE's vocabulary-building process visible. Then we will train the actual byte-level tokenizer with Hugging Face's tokenizers library, save it as a reusable artifact, and test it on medical text that was not used for training.

Medical corpus
          ↓
        Prepare and clean text
          ↓
        Train tokenizer
          ↓
        Learn vocabulary + BPE merge rules
          ↓
        Save tokenizer
          ↓
        Test on unseen medical text
          ↓
        Compare against a general tokenizer
          ↓
        Measure tokenization efficiency

We will compare token counts, tokens per word, and the fragmentation of representative medical terms. These measurements tell us about representation efficiency; a downstream model evaluation would be needed to make claims about task performance.

02 Data comes first

Your corpus is the design decision.

BPE learns recurring adjacent pieces of text, not medical definitions. Adjacent means next to each other in the text: BPE counts patterns such as tr, then tro, then longer sequences when those pairs repeatedly occur together. It does not know that troponin is a cardiac biomarker; it only notices which character or byte sequences appear frequently.

Imagine a small training corpus containing these lines:

troponin level was measured
high troponin level
repeat troponin test

In a simplified illustration, frequent neighboring pieces might gradually combine like this:

t + r       → tr
tr + o      → tro
tro + ponin → troponin

The final result could encode troponin as one piece instead of several smaller pieces. This is only a schematic example: the actual pieces depend on the complete corpus, vocabulary budget, pre-tokenizer, and learned merge rankings.

If the term is rare, missing, or written in several forms such as troponin, Trop I, and troponin-I, the same pattern may not occur often enough to merge. A general tokenizer might therefore split troponin into pieces such as tro, pon, and in, while a medical tokenizer might learn a longer piece. That does not mean the medical tokenizer understands the term; it has learned a more frequent representation of its spelling. An uncommon abbreviation or a new laboratory value may still be split into several pieces.

Why does the corpus change the vocabulary?

Consider two simplified training corpora. A general corpus might repeat patterns such as the cat is sleeping and the weather is nice. A medical corpus might repeat troponin level was measured and myocardial infarction was confirmed. Because BPE learns from frequency, the two tokenizers have different opportunities to create useful pieces.

A general tokenizer might represent troponin as ["tro", "pon", "in"], while a tokenizer trained heavily on medical text might represent it as ["troponin"] or ["trop", "onin"]. The exact result depends on the full corpus, vocabulary size, pre-tokenization strategy, and merge rankings. We do not tell BPE which terms to memorize; we give it representative text in which useful patterns can become frequent.

Abbreviations, measurements, and repeated terminology

Medical text also contains patterns that general English may see less often:

BRCA1    HbA1c    troponin-I
      5 mg     250 mg/dL    5 µg
      SOB      BP      HR

Frequency helps, but it does not guarantee one token. If troponin appears 100,000 times while troponin-I appears only 20 times, the tokenizer may learn a useful piece for the first form while still splitting the second. An abbreviation such as SOB is not automatically special just because it matters to a clinician; if it is rare in the corpus, BPE has little reason to create a dedicated token.

The same principle applies to recurring biomedical phrases. A medical corpus may repeatedly contain myocardial infarction, myocardial injury, and myocardial ischemia, giving BPE more opportunities to learn reusable pieces around myocardial. It is responding to the training distribution, not understanding cardiology.

Match the corpus to the language you care about

The medtok training path uses PubMed abstracts, which are useful for biomedical research prose but do not represent every kind of medical language.

Research paper:  Elevated troponin concentrations were associated with increased cardiovascular risk.
      Clinical note:   Pt c/o SOB. Troponin elevated. Repeat ECG.
      Prescription:    Take 5 mg twice daily.
      Conversation:     I've been having trouble breathing.

All four examples are medical-related, but their language is different. A tokenizer trained mostly on PubMed may learn scientific terminology while seeing relatively little shorthand, dosage instructions, or everyday patient language. The best corpus is therefore not simply the largest corpus; it is a corpus that resembles the text the tokenizer will process later. Evaluate on unseen data from that actual target domain.

Start with a data contract

Before training the tokenizer, define what your training data should look like. A simple text corpus might use UTF-8 text with one document per line:

UTF-8 text
      one document per line

Or it might use JSONL, with one JSON object per line and a non-empty text field:

{"text": "An authored example about myocardial infarction."}
      {"text": "An authored example about blood glucose measurement."}

A practical data contract should specify:

  • Encoding: UTF-8.
  • Format: one document per line, or JSONL with a non-empty text field.
  • Source: where the text came from.
  • License: whether it can legally be used for training and redistribution.
  • Download date: when the data was collected.
  • Filtering rules: what was removed and why.
  • Document identifiers: when available, so processing can be reproduced.
  • Deduplication: avoid training on the same document many times.
  • Language: decide which languages are included.
  • Document length: check for extremely short or extremely long documents.

These JSONL records are illustrative and contain no patient data. The source reader strips surrounding whitespace and skips blank lines; malformed JSON may fall back to raw text. Validate JSONL before training rather than relying on that permissive fallback.

Also preserve information that may matter to the domain:

BRCA1
      5 mg
      5 µg
      HbA1c
      troponin-I

These strings should not automatically be normalized into generic English text. 5 mg and 5 µg are different measurements. BRCA1 and BRCA2 are different identifiers. troponin-I and troponin are not necessarily interchangeable strings.

Preprocessing is therefore part of tokenizer design, not just a cleanup step. Every normalization rule can change which patterns become frequent and which distinctions remain available to the tokenizer.

A practical warning about medical data

Medical text is not automatically safe to publish.

Do not put patient identifiers, private clinical notes, or restricted datasets into a public repository simply because they are useful for tokenizer training.

Before using a dataset, verify:

  • Whether you are allowed to use it for training.
  • Whether redistribution is permitted.
  • Whether it contains personal or protected information.
  • Whether de-identification requirements have been satisfied.

A tokenizer vocabulary is not a privacy guarantee. A tokenizer can contain pieces derived from sensitive training data. Dataset governance and privacy requirements still apply even when you are only building a tokenizer.

The main lesson: a tokenizer can only learn patterns that its corpus gives it an opportunity to learn. Corpus selection is therefore one of the most important design decisions in the project.

03 Protect the experiment

Split before the vocabulary learns.

There is another important rule when training a tokenizer:

Do not let the tokenizer learn from the same documents you later use to evaluate it.

This is similar to a student taking an exam. If you give the student the exact exam questions before the exam, the score does not tell you how well they learned the material.

Study material → Exam → Score

Tokenizer training has a similar problem. BPE learns frequent patterns from its training corpus, so the evaluation documents should be unseen when the vocabulary is learned.

Training documents
      ↓
    Learn BPE vocabulary + merges
      ↓
    Evaluate on unseen documents

Why does this matter for BPE?

Suppose a small training corpus contains documents about influenza:

Influenza A infection can cause fever and cough.

    Patients with influenza may experience fever,
    cough, fatigue, and muscle aches.

    Seasonal influenza vaccination can reduce the
    risk of severe influenza infection.

While learning its vocabulary, BPE sees the word and character patterns in these documents. It may learn reusable pieces such as influenza, infection, or vaccination.

If we use these same documents to evaluate the tokenizer, we might report a result such as:

Before:  42 tokens
    After:   35 tokens

But that result does not tell us how well the tokenizer works on new medical text. The tokenizer has already seen the evaluation documents while learning its vocabulary.

Instead, keep a held-out set aside until evaluation:

TRAINING DATA
      ↓
    Learn vocabulary + merge rules
      ↓
    HELD-OUT DATA
      ↓
    Measure tokenization efficiency

This gives us a better answer to: Does this tokenizer work on medical text it has not seen before?

Example: 50,000 PubMed rows

Suppose the source file contains exactly 50,000 PubMed rows. We can use 45,000 rows to learn the vocabulary and keep 5,000 rows for evaluation:

uv run python scripts/split_corpus.py --heldout-n 5000 --seed 42
50,000PubMed rows
→
45,000Train / learn merges
5,000Held out / evaluate

These counts are illustrative. The script reserves at most 5,000 rows; a smaller corpus can leave no training rows. The --seed 42 option makes the shuffle reproducible: the same input and seed should produce the same split.

The 45,000 training rows are the only rows used to learn the tokenizer vocabulary and merge rules. The 5,000 held-out rows are kept aside until evaluation.

But a row split is not automatically a clean split

The script shuffles line records. It does not automatically deduplicate abstracts, detect near-duplicate documents, group related documents, create a time-based split, or guarantee that held-out text is independent of training text.

For example, these two rows have different row numbers but contain exactly the same information:

Row 101:   Influenza A infection was associated with fever and cough.
    Row 42317: Influenza A infection was associated with fever and cough.

A similar problem occurs with near-duplicates:

Training: Influenza A infection commonly causes fever and cough.
    Held out: Influenza A infection commonly causes fever, cough,
          and other respiratory symptoms.

If one version is used for training and the other for evaluation, the tokenizer has effectively seen much of the evaluation text already. A stronger preprocessing pipeline is:

Raw corpus
        ↓
    Remove duplicates / inspect overlap
        ↓
    Split into train / validation / test
        ↓
    Train tokenizer only on train

For vocabulary tuning, use three sets

A simple train/held-out split is enough to demonstrate the basic medtok workflow. Once we compare vocabulary sizes or preprocessing choices, use three sets with separate jobs:

  • Training: learn BPE merges and the vocabulary.
  • Validation: compare vocabulary sizes, preprocessing rules, token counts, tokens per word, term fragmentation, abbreviations, and units.
  • Test: run the final evaluation once after the configuration is frozen.
TRAIN
      ↓
    Learn vocabulary
      ↓
    Try tokenizer configurations
      ↓
    VALIDATION → choose vocabulary size / preprocessing
      ↓
    Freeze decisions
      ↓
    TEST → final reported result

For example, validation can compare 8,000-, 16,000-, and 32,000-token targets without repeatedly tuning against the final test set. Do not keep checking the test result and changing the tokenizer based on it; the test set would gradually become another validation set.

For a 50,000-row corpus, one possible three-way split is 45,000 training documents, 2,500 validation documents, and 2,500 test documents. The exact numbers are less important than keeping the roles separate.

The key idea

Your corpus determines what the tokenizer can learn. Your evaluation data must remain unseen while the tokenizer is learning. Together, a representative corpus, duplicate inspection, a clean train split, validation for design choices, and a final test set make the result more credible.

04 A tokenizer is a pipeline

Bytes in. Learned pieces out.

Now we can put the pieces together. A byte-level BPE tokenizer has two different jobs:

TRAINING                         ENCODING
    Medical corpus                  New text
      ↓                              ↓
    Convert text to bytes           Convert text to bytes
      ↓                              ↓
    Count frequent pairs             Apply saved merges
      ↓                              ↓
    Learn merge rules                Convert pieces to IDs
      ↓                              ↓
    Build vocabulary                 Return token IDs

The most important distinction is:

Training learns the vocabulary and merge rules. Encoding only uses what was already learned.

When you encode a new sentence, the tokenizer does not stop and learn a new vocabulary for that sentence. If training learned cardio + myopathy → cardiomyopathy, then encoding The patient has cardiomyopathy. simply applies the saved vocabulary and merge rules. It does not train itself again because the sentence is new.

The four stages

1. INPUT — preserve the text

The tokenizer starts with ordinary text:

The patient has cardiomyopathy.

In this configuration, no tokenizer normalizer is configured. The tokenizer therefore does not automatically turn CARDIOMYOPATHY into cardiomyopathy or apply Unicode normalization before tokenization.

There is one separate detail in the data pipeline: the training-file reader strips whitespace from the beginning and end of each input line. These concerns should not be confused:

Tokenizer normalizer
        ≠
    Training-file preprocessing

The first is part of tokenizer configuration. The second is part of how we prepare the training data.

2. BOUNDARIES — ByteLevel pre-tokenizer

Next, the text goes through the ByteLevel pre-tokenizer:

"The patient has cardiomyopathy."
            ↓
        UTF-8 byte representation
            ↓
        ByteLevel processing
            ↓
       pieces ready for BPE

The tokenizer starts from bytes, not from a fixed medical dictionary. UTF-8 can represent English, non-Latin text, and emoji using bytes, so the tokenizer does not need a separate base vocabulary containing every possible Unicode character. In this configuration, we also do not add an artificial leading space; that matters because ByteLevel tokenizers can use spaces as part of their token representation.

3. LEARNING — BPE learns vocabulary and merges

During training, BPE repeatedly looks for frequent adjacent pairs in the byte-level representation. Suppose a tiny medical corpus contains cardiomyopathy several times and, for illustration, BPE has already learned cardio and myopathy. If those pieces frequently occur next to each other, BPE can learn:

cardio + myopathy
        ↓
    cardiomyopathy

That merge is stored as part of the tokenizer's ranked merge rules. The actual order and pieces depend on the complete training corpus and vocabulary size; this is a simplified illustration, not a trace of the medtok learned merge order.

It is useful to think of the saved tokenizer as two things:

Vocabulary
    Which token pieces exist?

    Merge rules
    Which smaller pieces can be combined, and in what order?

During training, BPE discovers these rules from frequency. During encoding, it uses the saved rules.

4. OUTPUT — token pieces become IDs

The model does not directly receive strings such as cardio and myopathy. The tokenizer maps each piece to an integer ID:

cardiomyopathy
      ↓
    ["cardiomyopathy"]
      ↓
    [15482]

If the complete word was not learned as one piece, it might become ["cardio", "myopathy"] and then [8231, 9174]. These numbers are token IDs used to look up embeddings. An ID of 15482 is not more medical or more important than an ID of 8231; the numbers themselves have no semantic meaning.

When IDs need to become text again, the tokenizer uses its vocabulary and byte-level decoder:

Token IDs → token pieces → bytes → UTF-8 text

The goal is to reconstruct the original text as faithfully as the tokenizer configuration allows. See the pipeline reference →

Why start with all 256 bytes?

The initial byte alphabet contains all 256 possible byte values, from 0 through 255. That gives the tokenizer a basic representation for arbitrary valid UTF-8 input:

256 base byte symbols
        +
    learned BPE merges
        ↓
    larger final vocabulary

This does not mean there are only 256 final tokens. Training can learn larger pieces for common sequences such as the, patient, cardio, myocardial, and infection.

An English character such as A uses one byte in UTF-8, while characters such as é, 中, and an emoji use multiple bytes. Therefore, byte-level tokenization does not mean one character equals one token. A character may initially become several byte symbols, and BPE may later merge some of them.

What is Ġ?

When inspecting a ByteLevel BPE vocabulary, you may see pieces such as Ġthe, Ġpatient, or Ġcardio. The Ġ is a convention used by GPT-2-style ByteLevel tokenizers to represent a space-related boundary. It is not an extra letter in the patient's text.

Do not manually reconstruct text by joining displayed vocabulary strings. Let the tokenizer's decoder convert token IDs back through the vocabulary, bytes, and UTF-8 text.

The complete pipeline

TRAINING
    Medical corpus
      ↓
    UTF-8 bytes
      ↓
    ByteLevel BPE
      ↓
    Count frequent byte pairs
      ↓
    Learn merge rules
      ↓
    Vocabulary + merges
      ↓
    tokenizer.json

    ENCODING
    New medical text
      ↓
    UTF-8 bytes
      ↓
    Apply saved merges
      ↓
    Token pieces
      ↓
    Token IDs
      ↓
    Transformer model

Once training is finished, the tokenizer is frozen. The central idea is simple:

Training discovers the tokenizer. Encoding uses the tokenizer.

The tokenizer does not learn something new every time it sees a sentence.

05 The actual training recipe

Train your medical BPE.

The core configuration below follows the medtok trainer. The library implements BPE; you do not need to implement its merge-counting engine yourself.

# Core tokenizer object, BPE model, and byte-level encode/decode helpers.
from tokenizers import Tokenizer, decoders, models
from tokenizers import pre_tokenizers, processors, trainers

# Start with an empty BPE model. Training will learn its vocabulary and merges.
tokenizer = Tokenizer(models.BPE())

# Convert input text into byte-level pieces before BPE applies its merge rules.
# False keeps the input's leading-space behavior unchanged.
tokenizer.pre_tokenizer = pre_tokenizers.ByteLevel(add_prefix_space=False)

# Convert byte-level pieces back into the original text during decoding.
tokenizer.decoder = decoders.ByteLevel()

# Clean up byte-level span offsets for reporting; this does not remove spaces.
tokenizer.post_processor = processors.ByteLevel(trim_offsets=True)

# Define how the vocabulary and merge rules should be learned.
trainer = trainers.BpeTrainer(
    # Target vocabulary size, including the byte alphabet and special tokens.
    vocab_size=16000,
    # Ignore candidate pairs that occur fewer than two times.
    min_frequency=2,
    # Reserve IDs for end-of-text and padding markers.
    special_tokens=["<|endoftext|>", "<pad>"],
    # Start with all 256 byte values so arbitrary UTF-8 text is representable.
    initial_alphabet=pre_tokenizers.ByteLevel.alphabet(),
)

vocab_size is a target, including the initial alphabet and special tokens. A small corpus or frequency cutoff can stop training before that target. min_frequency=2 is the minimum pair frequency for a merge, not a guarantee that every final token appears twice in encoded documents.

trim_offsets=True adjusts span offsets associated with byte-level pieces; it does not delete spaces from decoded text. Registering EOS and padding tokens allocates IDs, but does not by itself append EOS to each sequence.

uv run python scripts/train_medical_tokenizer.py --corpus data/pubmed_train.jsonl --out artifacts/medical-bpe-pubmed/tokenizer.json --vocab-size 16000 --min-frequency 2

uv run python scripts/train_medical_tokenizer.py --corpus data/general_train.jsonl --out artifacts/general-bpe/tokenizer.json --vocab-size 16000 --min-frequency 2

The trainer streams batches of non-empty documents through train_from_iterator, creates the output directory, and writes a serialized tokenizer.json. Record the actual vocabulary size printed at the end of each run.

A smaller first run

The implementation also includes an authored corpus and a tiny fallback artifact. Run the default trainer for a quick local smoke test:

uv run python scripts/train_medical_tokenizer.py

This writes artifacts/medical-bpe/tokenizer.json, a different path from the PubMed-trained artifact. Do not label its results as full PubMed evaluation.

Colab playbook: medical 6k and GPT-5

The project's custom medical tokenization playbook on Google Colab loads a medical 6k tokenizer and applies it to the same text as an optional GPT-5 tokenizer baseline. The implementation repository used to build the custom medical tokenizer is gaurav36/med-tok on GitHub. The repository contains the implementation; the Hugging Face repository below supplies the tokenizer artifact loaded by this example.

Load a trained tokenizer, then apply it to new text

PreTrainedTokenizerFast provides the Transformers interface to a fast tokenizer backed by the tokenizers library. from_pretrained() loads its saved vocabulary, merge rules, and configuration from the Hub or a local cache. It does not train the tokenizer again or load a language model. In Colab, install the dependency if needed:

%pip install transformers

Use a descriptive variable name for the medical artifact. That keeps the notebook readable even when cells run out of order.

from transformers import PreTrainedTokenizerFast

    medical_6k = PreTrainedTokenizerFast.from_pretrained(
      "gaurav36/medical-bpe-6k"
    )
    text = "Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors are used in Alzheimer's disease treatment."

    print(medical_6k.tokenize(text))

The published artifact is medical-bpe-6k. Loading uncached files requires internet access and permission to access the repository. The sentence is an illustrative tokenization input, not treatment guidance.

Compare with the encoding selected for GPT-5

Use tiktoken.encoding_for_model("gpt-5") to select GPT-5's text encoding according to the installed tiktoken version. This loads encoding data, not GPT-5 weights, and does not call the OpenAI API or require an API key. Initial installation and uncached encoding downloads need internet access. This is an external tokenizer baseline, not a same-recipe general BPE control.

%pip install -U tiktoken
import tiktoken  # Install with: %pip install -U tiktoken

# Select the text encoding associated with the model name.
# This loads tokenizer data, not GPT-5 weights or an API connection.
gpt5_encoding = tiktoken.encoding_for_model("gpt-5")
text = "Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors are used in Alzheimer's disease treatment."

# Convert the input string into integer vocabulary IDs.
gpt5_ids = gpt5_encoding.encode(text)

print("Encoding:", gpt5_encoding.name)
print("Token IDs:")
print(gpt5_ids)

# Show a readable view of each token's exact bytes.
# A token can split a multi-byte UTF-8 character, so replacement characters
# here are only a display issue; decode the complete sequence to verify text.
gpt5_pieces = [
    gpt5_encoding.decode_single_token_bytes(token_id).decode(
        "utf-8", errors="replace"
    )
    for token_id in gpt5_ids
]
print("\nTokens:")
print(gpt5_pieces)

# Count tokens and verify that encoding followed by complete decoding
# reconstructs the original input exactly.
print("Token count:", len(gpt5_ids))
assert gpt5_encoding.decode(gpt5_ids) == text

The variable gpt5_ids holds integers; gpt5_pieces is a readable byte-decoded view. Spaces in this view appear as actual spaces, such as " inhibitors", rather than the Hugging Face raw-piece marker Ġ. For other inputs, individual tokens can split a UTF-8 character: errors="replace" can then produce replacement characters in this diagnostic view. Decode the complete ID sequence to check reconstruction, as shown above.

Inspect the resulting pieces

The 6k tokenizer's output shows how this saved vocabulary represents the example sentence. A single sentence is useful for inspection, but it is not a benchmark and cannot establish better medical reasoning, latency, or overall model quality.

What does the space marker mean?

Ġ is the visible byte-level representation used for a space in these token strings. For example, Ġinhibitors represents a piece that includes the preceding space. It is not a literal extra letter in the input. Case and leading spaces affect segmentation: changing Acetylcholinesterase to lowercase or placing it after a space can change the pieces. Keep input strings identical when comparing tokenizers.

From pieces to IDs, and back

For the Hugging Face medical tokenizer, tokenize() exposes the pieces; calling the tokenizer returns an encoding with input_ids and auxiliary fields such as an attention mask. Each ID is an index in this tokenizer's vocabulary, not a medical concept number or a score. The snippet makes special-token exclusion explicit.

encoding = medical_6k(text, add_special_tokens=False)
print("Token IDs:", encoding["input_ids"])
print("Token pieces:", medical_6k.convert_ids_to_tokens(encoding["input_ids"]))

convert_ids_to_tokens() gives the raw piece representations, including space markers. To reconstruct readable text, use the matching tokenizer's decoder. This is a check to run in the notebook, not an independently executed result:

decoded = medical_6k.decode(
    encoding["input_ids"],
    skip_special_tokens=False,
    clean_up_tokenization_spaces=False,
)
print(decoded)
assert decoded == text

For reproducibility, record len(medical_6k), the Transformers version, and the Hub revision used to load the artifact. An unpinned repository name can change over time. Encoding this plain sentence does not count the complete overhead of a GPT-5 API conversation, tools, or other request content.

What this playbook demonstrates

08 Lab / vocabulary budget

A larger vocabulary has a bill.

BPE learns additional pieces through merges. If we allow more merges, the tokenizer can learn more specialized medical pieces. A larger vocabulary can therefore make token sequences shorter, but it also makes a compatible language model larger.

For example, a smaller vocabulary might represent electrocardiogram as three tokens, ["electro", "cardio", "gram"], while a larger vocabulary might learn the whole word as one token, ["electrocardiogram"]. In this illustrative example, the second tokenizer uses two fewer tokens for the word. A larger vocabulary does not guarantee this result for every term, and that specialized entry is not free.

Why not make the vocabulary huge?

A huge vocabulary is not automatically better. Some newly learned pieces may be useful and frequent, such as electrocardiogram, but many additional entries may save only a small number of tokens. Other specialized medical terms may appear only a few times in the training corpus. A rare token has less evidence from which to learn a useful embedding, even though it still consumes a vocabulary row and increases the model's memory cost.

The tradeoff: larger vocabulary → more specialized pieces → potentially shorter sequences, but also more parameters and more rare tokens. There is no universal correct vocabulary size for medical text; the appropriate size depends on the corpus, language, tokenizer design, model architecture, and intended task.

A language model needs a vector representation for every token in its vocabulary. Suppose the vocabulary has 16,000 entries and the embedding dimension is 1,024. The input embedding matrix then contains:

What is the knee of the fertility curve?

Suppose we train several medical tokenizers with different vocabulary sizes and test them on the same unseen medical documents. A smaller vocabulary may split electrocardiogram into ["electro", "cardio", "gram"], while a larger vocabulary may learn ["electrocardiogram"]. Increasing the vocabulary can therefore reduce the number of tokens needed to represent the same text.

This is what fertility measures: how many tokens are used per word on average. If a document contains 100 words and the tokenizer produces 150 tokens:

Fertility150 ÷ 100 = 1.5 tokens per word

A fertility of 1.5 means that each word becomes 1.5 tokens on average. Lower fertility means fewer tokens represent the same text.

But bigger is not always worth it

Going from 16k to 32k saves 50 tokens per document, which is substantial. Going from 32k to 100k saves only 5 more tokens per document. We would be adding tens of thousands of vocabulary entries for a relatively small additional reduction.

The knee, also called the elbow, is the region where the curve drops quickly at first and then becomes flatter. It is not simply "choose the vocabulary with the lowest fertility." The practical question is: how much additional vocabulary and model memory am I paying for the remaining reduction in token count?

The knee is a trade-off, not a magic number

There is no universal vocabulary size where the knee must occur. It might be around 32k for one corpus, 50k for another, or even 100k. The result depends on the corpus, language, domain, and tokenizer configuration.

Lower fertility also does not prove that a model will perform better on a medical task. A tokenizer producing 1.25 tokens per word is more efficient than one producing 1.35, but that does not tell us which model will be better at diagnosis prediction, question answering, information extraction, summarization, or medical reasoning. Those require separate downstream evaluations.

A simple rule used by the script

Instead of trying to calculate the exact geometric curvature, the script uses a simpler near-best rule: choose the smallest vocabulary whose mean token count is within 2% of the best observed result.

2% thresholdacceptable mean tokens/document ≤ best mean × 1.02

For the illustrative sweep, the best result is 205 tokens per document, so the threshold is 205 × 1.02 = 209.10. The 16k result at 260 fails, 32k at 210 narrowly fails, and 50k at 207 passes. The script therefore selects 50k, the smallest candidate within 2% of the best result.

The visual curve might make 32k look like the knee because most of the improvement happened before that point. However, 32k is above the 209.10 threshold. This shows that the visual knee and the script's 2% selection rule are not necessarily the same. The 2% rule is a practical, tunable heuristic, not a mathematical proof of an optimal vocabulary.

Why use mean tokens per document?

You may wonder why the sweep uses mean tokens per document instead of fertility directly. On the same fixed evaluation corpus:

Mean tokens/documenttotal tokens ÷ number of documentsFertilitytotal tokens ÷ total words

Both measures are directly proportional to the total number of tokens. Therefore, a relative threshold such as 2% gives the same selection using either unrounded metric, as long as every tokenizer is evaluated on the same unseen documents. If the evaluation corpus changes, the numbers are no longer directly comparable because differences may come from the text rather than the tokenizer.

How to obtain a trustworthy knee

  1. Prepare disjoint training and validation data. Use training text for --corpus and validation text for --heldout, as in the command above. Verify document-content separation after deduplication. Keep a separate final test set; data already used to select settings is validation data.
  2. Audit cached candidates before running. Record each artifact's actual size, hash, corpus provenance, and preprocessing. Preserve existing artifacts; use separate output paths for a clean sweep and check the implementation's help for supported cache/output controls. Correcting input paths alone does not prevent cache reuse.
  3. Compare one controlled recipe. Keep training corpus, preprocessing, frequency threshold, and evaluation set fixed while changing the target. Obtain an actual 16k artifact if claiming a 16k-versus-32k comparison. Label saturated candidates by their actual sizes.
  4. Select on validation, then report once on test. Apply the 2% tolerance to unrounded means, weigh actual memory and out-of-domain costs, and evaluate the selected artifact on untouched test data. Evaluate downstream model quality separately before claiming a better language model.

10 The boundary that matters

New tokenizer. Not a drop-in model upgrade.

A pretrained model associates each token ID with a learned embedding. In a new vocabulary, the same ID may refer to a different piece. Matching vocabulary sizes or resizing embeddings does not repair that semantic mismatch.

EXISTING MODEL

Keep its tokenizer.

For ordinary fine-tuning or LoRA, use the model's stock tokenizer and formatting. Domain adaptation does not require replacing its vocabulary.

YOUR TOKENIZER

Plan model training.

Train a compatible model from scratch, or undertake a separately evaluated vocabulary-adaptation procedure with embedding initialization and continued training.

Changing tokenizer files alone is neither of those procedures. Treat medtok as a tokenizer training and measurement experiment, not proof that a pretrained LLM has gained medical expertise.

Paper: How Good is Your Tokenizer? On the Monolingual Performance of Multilingual Language Models

Core claim

This paper asks why monolingual models often beat multilingual ones on single-language tasks. The authors argue that the answer is not simply "monolingual is smarter." A large share of the difference comes from two concrete factors: how much training data the model saw in that language and how well the tokenizer fits that language.

Main conclusion: multilingual models are often not weaker by design. They are frequently disadvantaged because they must process some languages with less suitable token pieces.

How the paper reasons about the problem

The paper treats tokenization as part of the model, not as a small preprocessing detail. The authors compare multilingual BERT with monolingual BERT models across nine languages and five tasks, then measure whether the multilingual tokenizer breaks words into too many fragments. They use measures such as fertility, the average number of subword pieces per word, and the proportion of words that get split across multiple pieces.

The logic is straightforward: if a word that could have been represented as one meaningful unit is instead broken into many fragments, the model must reconstruct meaning from a noisier input. That increases sequence length and can make morphology harder to learn, especially in languages where suffixes and internal word structure carry a lot of information.

Simple example

Imagine a medical or technical word that appears often in one language. A tokenizer specialized for that language may learn a compact, stable representation for it, while a shared multilingual tokenizer may split it into several smaller fragments because its vocabulary budget is spread across many languages. The model then sees the same concept through more pieces, more positions, and more chances for ambiguity. The paper's point is that this difference is not cosmetic; it can change downstream task performance.

A useful picture is limited shelf space. A monolingual tokenizer gives most of the shelf to one language. A multilingual tokenizer must share that same shelf across many languages. English, Japanese, or Chinese may already be represented reasonably well, but languages like Finnish, Turkish, Korean, and Arabic are more likely to lose useful vocabulary coverage and end up with longer, more fragmented token sequences.

What the experiments show

The strongest part of the paper is the controlled experiment. The authors train new models on the same language data but swap tokenizers, which lets them separate the effect of training data size from the effect of tokenization. The pattern is clear: specialized monolingual tokenizers usually improve downstream results, especially for Finnish, Turkish, Korean, and Arabic. For English, Japanese, and Chinese, where the multilingual tokenizer already fits better, the gap is much smaller.

Why it matters here

For this project, the paper is a reminder that fewer token pieces can be meaningful evidence about tokenizer quality, but only when the comparison is controlled. A domain tokenizer can help because it learns better pieces for the text you care about, not because token count alone proves a better model.

Tokenization Is More Than Compression

Paper: Tokenization Is More Than Compression

This paper pushes back on a tempting idea: if a tokenizer turns text into fewer tokens, it should automatically be better. The authors build that challenge into the experiment itself by introducing PathPiece, a tokenizer whose segmentation explicitly tries to use the fewest possible tokens for a given vocabulary. If compression alone were the main reason tokenizers work well, this kind of setup should dominate.

Core claim

The authors argue that good tokenization is not just about compression. A tokenizer that produces fewer tokens does not automatically lead to better downstream performance. What matters is how the tokenizer is designed across three stages:

  1. Pre-tokenization
  2. Vocabulary construction
  3. Segmentation

Main conclusion: effective tokenization gives the model useful structure, not just shorter sequences.

What the paper actually tested

The authors trained a large set of language models while varying tokenizers, vocabulary-building methods, segmentation rules, and vocabulary sizes. Their most direct test was PathPiece itself: a tokenizer designed to minimize token count as aggressively as possible. If the simple rule "fewer tokens means better models" were true, PathPiece should consistently win. It does not.

Across their experiments, several tokenizers perform competitively, and no single method dominates purely because it compresses more. In fact, some choices that reduce token count too aggressively produce awkward token pieces, especially when boundaries around spaces are not handled carefully. That hurts the quality of the representation even if the sequence gets shorter.

Simple example

The paper gives a useful intuition here. Suppose a tokenizer is allowed to chase the smallest possible token count with very weak boundary constraints. It might create pieces that cross word boundaries or bundle part of one word with part of the next. That looks efficient on paper because the sequence is shorter, but it gives the model a less natural unit to learn from. The model saves tokens and loses clarity.

That is why the paper separates three stages: pre-tokenization decides where boundaries are allowed, vocabulary construction decides which pieces exist, and segmentation decides how those pieces are used on real text. A tokenizer can look good on the compression metric while making poor choices at one of those stages.

Why this matters

That result is important because it separates efficiency from usefulness. A tokenizer can save tokens but still make the model's job harder by breaking text into unnatural pieces. The paper shows that preserving cleaner structure, especially around word boundaries and the way vocabularies are initialized, can matter as much as raw compression.

The practical message is useful for this project: token count is only one symptom. A tokenizer that aggressively compresses text can still produce unnatural pieces that make the model's job harder. The paper's broader conclusion is that effective tokenization is about giving the model a representation that is efficient and structurally helpful, not merely short.

Why it matters here

For medtok, this is a guardrail against overclaiming. If a custom medical tokenizer uses fewer pieces, that is interesting, but it is not sufficient evidence by itself. The real question is whether the vocabulary creates cleaner, more useful units for the model and whether that advantage survives controlled evaluation.

Quick glossary
Vocabulary
The mapping between token pieces and integer IDs.
Merge rule
A learned instruction to combine an adjacent pair of pieces, applied according to its rank.
Fertility
The ratio of total tokens to total whitespace-delimited words in this experiment.
Byte-level BPE
BPE over a reversible byte alphabet, with a pre-tokenizer defining eligible chunks.
Held-out set
Data excluded from training. If used to choose settings, it is validation data, not an untouched final test.
